# Make a proposal testable and feasible

Use for a research, interpretive or design proposal; scale to the requested artifact. This adds traceability to [writing modes](modes.md), not another compulsory interview or empirical template. Reuse the intended readers, genre, source boundary and destination. A prospective project has no observed results merely because a polished proposal needs an ending.

## Start with the available material

Prefer the least-processed available evidence: full source passages and their methods/limitations, the actual protocol and amendments, primary texts with editions/locators, raw data and analysis code if any work has occurred, and current institution/funder/venue instructions. Summaries and existing prose help orientation but cannot establish access, method adequacy or completed work. Ask for a missing item only when it changes what can honestly be proposed. Missing access can support a conditional plan or a smaller feasible question.

## Trace the proposed contribution

For each consequential question or aim, complete a compact internal map; include it in the deliverable when useful or requested:

| Question | Proposed contribution | Required evidence/access | Method | Planned analysis/argument | Feasible deliverable and limit |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Read across each row: would the available evidence and method answer that question, would the analysis justify the contribution, and can the stated deliverable actually be produced? Read backwards too: every promised output needs a method and evidence. A method name alone is not an analysis plan. Specify the comparison, inference or interpretive steps that connect evidence to the proposed answer. Reuse source/claim IDs rather than inventing a second evidence database.

Keep proposed contributions, expected outcomes, illustrative examples and observed findings distinct at their point of use. Expected does not mean guaranteed. A proposal without collected data should deliver a protocol, analysis plan, argument outline or other feasible prospective artifact, not a Results section populated with invented numbers.

## Challenge the weakest dependency

Identify the material assumptions, strongest alternative approach or account, and a concrete result, passage, objection or access failure that would weaken or remove the proposed contribution. Explain the decision that would follow: narrow the claim, change the design, justify replication, or stop. Choose a challenge appropriate to the inquiry; an interpretive proposal need not invent an experiment to count as rigorous.

Separate evidence currently accessible from evidence that requires permission, recruitment, equipment, expertise, funding or another prerequisite. Check time and resource bounds, dependencies and the smallest informative next step. If causal claims need visitor measurements but only museum labels are accessible, do not quietly substitute textual counts as evidence of recall. Offer a feasible textual question or make the visitor study explicitly conditional.

Use a conceptual framework when it clarifies constructs and relations, a hypothesis when the design warrants a prediction, and a timeline when sequencing/resources matter. Do not mandate these as universal sections. A timeline should connect actual dependencies to deliverables and decision points, not manufacture dates or approvals. Keep specific limitations and alternatives visible; a generic "more research is needed" is insufficient.

Before drafting the final proposal, test its contribution against [the prewriting gate](prewriting.md). Finish with the warranted artifact and explicit unresolved dependencies. Planning a study never authorizes running it.
